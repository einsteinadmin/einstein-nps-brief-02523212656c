1 · The case

What actually happened

Brittany A., Fort Worth, August 26. Her complete GatherUp timeline:

  • 8/24 — customer added after her move
  • 8/25 — feedback request sent by SMS, delivered. No response.
  • 8/26 — 1st reminder sent — one day later. Delivered.
  • 8/26 — she opens the survey from the reminder, selects 5 of 10, and "Stop" gets recorded — logged by GatherUp as an SMS reply, with an unsubscribe stamped the same day.

Honest caveat on the mechanics: we can't tell from the log whether she texted the STOP keyword back or typed "stop" into the survey's comment box not knowing an unsubscribe option existed. Either way the message was the same — enough texts — and either way she first opened the survey and deliberately picked a 5. Her score is real. So is the feedback about our follow-up.

A 5 is a detractor, it lands in a small branch's quarterly number, and Fort Worth is already the tightest NPS story in the company. Hence the debate.

2 · The sides

What y'all are saying — steelmanned

Mike + Brian (the branch view)

Managers shouldn't be dinged for what they can't control. A single detractor moves a branch's quarter hard, and when the detractor is "I'm annoyed by your texts," coaching a team on it is impossible to defend with a straight face.

Fix the system first so this can't happen (Brian) — removal only if it happens anyway. And the credibility of a crackdown depends on the scoreboard being defensible.

Brian's second question: if a 2 gets service-recovered into a 10, does the 2 stick?

Cameron + Amanda + Matisen (the metric view)

She's a detractor and the score stays. Writing "stop" in the survey means she clicked, chose a number, and said something — deliberate acts (Matisen). Even "I hate your follow-up" is real feedback (Amanda).

Most companies don't curate their surveys. Start deleting "unfair" ones and the number stops meaning anything.

Base-rate question first (Cameron): how often does this actually happen? Nobody in the thread had a number. Now we do — Section 4.

Gerald's synthesis (via Mike)

The room is answering two different questions — "is she a detractor?" (yes) and "is this branch-attributable?" (no) — so nobody's actually disagreeing. One number is doing two jobs: measuring customer loyalty for the company and evaluating manager performance at the branch. Route, don't delete: root-cause tag every detractor, keep all of them in company NPS, count only attributable ones on the branch scorecard, add recovery-rate and response-rate as manager metrics, and fix the survey instrument so opt-outs can't enter the pipeline.

3 · The citations

What Reichheld actually says — fact-checked

Gerald's post leaned on Fred Reichheld (creator of NPS, The Ultimate Question 2.0) and his 2021 follow-up. Claim by claim:

"In 2021 he published Net Promoter 3.0, essentially conceding the metric had been corrupted" Partly holds

NP 3.0 (HBR, Nov–Dec 2021) is real, and Reichheld does describe scores getting gamed. But two precision points, verified against the sources. First: the warning isn't new to 3.0 — The Ultimate Question 2.0 (2011) already lists "Be Careful About Linking NPS to Compensation" among its requisites of success. Second: the mechanism NP 3.0 names is tying scores to frontline bonuses ("made them care more about their scores than about learning to better serve customers"). "Unit scorecards" appears in neither source — that phrase was Gerald's addition, and it's the phrase that made our branch scorecard look indicted. Earned Growth Rate is introduced as a "hard, complementary metric" — an accounting audit to pair with NPS, not a retraction. Reichheld remains the metric's biggest advocate.

"His closed-loop doctrine requires attribution — the score is a trigger to investigate, not a verdict" Holds

Accurate. The score was never supposed to be the deliverable; the root cause was. This is the strongest borrowed idea in Gerald's post, and the proposal adopts it (tags + callbacks, Section 5).

"At ~20 responses per branch per quarter, the confidence interval is ±20 — a 90 and a 72 are indistinguishable" Breaks on our data

Plain English on what this number even is: if a branch delivered the exact same quality all quarter and you could re-run that quarter over and over, the measured NPS would bounce around a bit each time, just based on which customers happen to answer the survey. The confidence interval is the size of that bounce, per branch, per quarter. The fewer responses, the bigger the bounce.

Gerald's ±20 assumed a branch gets ~20 responses a quarter. No Einstein branch is anywhere near that: the floor last quarter was Tampa at 44, the average ~120, Dallas 200. At our real volumes the bounce is roughly ±7. Still worth respecting (Section 4), but "a 90 and a 72 are statistically the same" is not true of any branch we run. One more thing worth stating plainly: neither of Reichheld's books publishes a minimum sample size — there's no "at least X responses per period counts" rule to cite, from him or against him. The math above is the honest guide.

"Anyone using Reichheld to defend a hard 90 threshold on a branch scorecard is invoking the practice he walked back" Breaks — built on the added phrase

This one leans entirely on the "unit scorecards" wording that isn't in Reichheld. What he actually warned against is paying people on the score — and nobody at Einstein is paid on NPS. Our scorecard use (graded against the 90 line, in bands, quarterly, as one evaluation input) is much closer to what his books recommend: use the score to find problems and close the loop. One honest observation stays: evaluation pressure creates its own softer gaming urge, and it looks like this thread — the push to remove scores. That's the argument for a hard no-removals rule, not against the scorecard.

Finding 1 — The problem we're debating is two reviews a year

Of 166 detractors, exactly two are artifacts a removal policy would target. That's 1.2%. Everything else is a customer with a real grievance. We nearly designed an adjudication court for two reviews a year.

Half of everything we know about unhappy customers arrives on the reminder — and those reminder-sourced detractors are the same genuine complaints as the initial-touch ones, not "stop texting me" protest votes. The reminder isn't up for debate in this brief: it stays. The 1-day timing also stays — asking while the move is fresh is why it converts, and stretching the gap trades real responses to spare roughly one annoyed customer a year. At two unsubscribes a year, the Stops are a cost of doing measurement, not a problem to engineer around.

The actual sequence, from GatherUp's Request Setup (checked on two branches — identical config)

Touch 1: initial rating request (Email/SMS) → Touch 2: First Rating Reminder, ON, sends 1 day after the initial → Touch 3: a "Second Rating Reminder" slot exists in GatherUp (would send 10 days after the first) but is toggled OFF on every branch. There is no third ask.

⚠️ Naming trap: the thread's "2nd follow-up" IS the First Rating Reminder — the second touch. In GatherUp's own terms the "second reminder" is already off; the thing the thread means produces half our responses. Nothing about the sequence changes: two touches, one day apart, as-is.

The concession Mike & Brian earn

At typical branch volume, one detractor moves the quarter 1.5–2.5 points, the noise band is ±7ish, the Green band is 3 points wide, and branches live 1–3 points off the 90 line. A single review genuinely can decide a band color at the margin. That's real, and pretending otherwise loses the room. Garland is the live case: 85 on 58 responses — one point below the Red line, one or two reviews from a different color.

The concession the data refuses

Dallas is not noise. 79 on 200 responses with 14 detractors — the most responses in the company, converging on the same score. The full-year detractor count agrees (Dallas leads with 29). The sample-size argument defends Garland's quarter; it cannot defend Dallas's. The worst score in the company is also the best-measured one.

A fairness note on branch size (Cameron's point)

Garland doesn't have fewer responses because the team is slacking — it runs fewer trucks and does fewer moves. Response count will never be equal across branches, and that's fine. The fair cross-branch comparison is response rate — responses per completed move — where Garland (21.4%) is solidly mid-pack and a small branch can absolutely beat a big one. Two different problems, two different fixes: a small branch's volatility is best handled with a longer measurement window (that's Ruling item 4); a branch's capture is handled by response rate (Ruling item 3). Nobody gets asked to conjure responses their move count can't produce.

Brian's ratio question — "for every detractor, how many promoters to move the score positive? Or is it variable?"

Both. In the NPS formula a promoter and a detractor are equal and opposite: one promoter exactly cancels one detractor (each is ±1 in the same numerator), so netting positive after a detractor lands takes two promoter responses where you'd otherwise have had passives. And yes, it's variable — how much each one moves the score depends entirely on response volume: at Houston's 273 responses a detractor costs 0.7 points; at Garland's 58 it costs 3.4. Same detractor, five times the damage — which is Finding 5 in one sentence: the denominator is the defense.

Finding 5 — The volatility fix is the response rate, and it's fully in the manager's control

Houston gets 53% of customers to respond. North Austin gets 8.7%. Same survey, same company — a 6× spread. Every point of response rate grows the denominator, shrinks the noise, and cuts the self-selection bias (angry customers respond regardless; happy ones need the nudge). You don't stabilize a branch's NPS by deleting detractors — you stabilize it by tripling the sample. Houston proves it's achievable.

Two free findings from reading all 166 comments

"The new / inexperienced mover" is the single most repeated complaint motif of the year. We named the quarter Training Camp before reading any of this. Receipts for the theme.

Three customers reported being promised follow-up that never came. The claims/callback loop is quietly manufacturing detractors on its own.

5 · Where it landed

The ruling, after everyone weighed in

Brian, Amanda and Mike all sent reactions. Five proposals went out; two were ratified unanimously, two were voted down, and one came back reshaped by the people who rejected it. What follows is the version to ratify — the original ballot and every response is preserved below it.

3 of 3 ratified item 1
3 of 3 ratified item 5
2 of 3 cut the routing layer
3 of 3 rejected per-branch bands
0 asked to delete a score
Ruling 1 · ratified 3 of 3

Nothing gets deleted. Nothing gets excluded. Company and branch numbers keep every score.

The one item nobody argued with — and the one where a mind actually changed. Amanda came in from the shield-the-branch side and reversed herself in writing: a genuinely delighted customer doesn't rate us a 5 over a text message. Brian got to the same place from the data: "where is that line drawn once you start curating the responses."

Ruling 2 · reshaped by Mike and Brian

Response timing becomes the manager metric. Recovery rate is cut.

Grading managers on whether an upset customer becomes happy failed 2 to 1 — Mike's objection is that a real share of detractors will never be satisfied no matter what we spend, so the outcome isn't the manager's to control. He then handed over the version that works: how fast a manager responds to a low score is entirely in their control. Brian, at 50/50, wanted exactly the same thing — "context representing the workload a manager does to respond."

Still to settle: the definition (hours to first logged response on a detractor) and where it lives. Response rate stays in as the free half — GatherUp already measures it, no build required.

Ruling 3 · replaces the band proposal

Mid-quarter reads are coaching. The grade stamps at three months. The fix for volatility is more responses, not a longer window.

All three rejected per-branch band widths, for the same reason: don't move the goalposts. Brian wanted scorecard consistency quarter to quarter; Amanda pointed out a small branch is in some ways harder, not easier; Mike said plainly the goalpost shouldn't differ by branch.

But the problem they were pointing at is real, and it isn't the bands. It's what a mid-quarter number gets used for. Mike's actual example: we walk into Fort Worth four weeks into a quarter, they've taken one bad review, and they hear that they're behind. That's demoralizing — and it's a framing failure, not a measurement one.

The ruling: a mid-quarter NPS read is a coaching update, not a grade. It sounds like "here's where your score sits today, here's your response rate, here's how to get more promoters before this gets stamped" — never "you're underperforming." The grade is stamped once, at the end of the quarter. No rolling six-month window — the full-year audit already showed a three-month window carries enough volume at every branch (company average ~120 responses a quarter, the smallest floor 44). The honest lever is response rate: North Austin sits at 8.7% while Houston pulls 53.1%. Closing that gap does more for score stability than any change to the ruler.

Ruling 4 · ratified 3 of 3

The 2 stays. After a logged recovery, we're allowed to invite an updated score.

Unanimous, and Mike sharpened it: the invitation comes after the recovery is attempted, never instead of it. A new higher score counterbalances the old detractor in the same formula — the number repairs itself when the recovery is real.

Dropped · 2 of 3 against

Gerald's root-cause routing layer — cut for complexity.

Brian: "complexity for complexity sake then I am out." Amanda: "too messy, especially when we have different people working in different branches at all times doing sales." Mike agreed with it but gave no reasoning. Two of the three people who would have to run it say it won't survive contact with the floor, so it doesn't ship. The problem it was meant to solve — routing fixes rather than arguing scores — is handled by response timing plus the existing claims flow.

Two open items before this is final

Amanda's overall vote was DISCUSS, not agree — though her written comment reads as agreement with the substance ("if we start picking and choosing reviews, then accountability gets lost and more excuses start to happen"). Worth confirming directly rather than assuming.

Brian volunteered to carry this down. His words: "if we can write this up and clearly define some of this and present that to our managers so everyone understands then I feel confident leading my managers on these principles." The manager one-pager exists — that's the ask he made, answered.

3

Reshaped

Gerald's best idea, upgraded by the data. Recovery rate measures the thing that would have saved the Fort Worth customer in Finding 4 — she told us in writing she'd have been fine if we'd gone into problem-solving mode, and nobody ever called her back. That save never happened, and today nothing on any scoreboard even notices. Response rate is the volatility fix (Finding 5): a manager who moves it from 9% to 30% does more for their score's stability than any deletion policy ever could.

How recovery gets counted (Cameron's question): a recovery = a logged callback + a resolution the customer accepted (claim settled, redo done, refund taken) — an operational record, like a closed claim. If we adopt it, it needs a home (candidates: a tag + note on the GatherUp customer record, a group on the claims/CET Monday board, or a simple shared log) and its accuracy will only ever be as good as branch self-reporting — though at ~14 detractors a month company-wide, this is a small, very auditable stream. Priority-wise it sits BEHIND response rate, which needs no build at all: GatherUp already measures it today.

Three months stays the grading period for every branch, and there is no rolling six-month window. The full-year audit already proved a quarter carries enough volume everywhere. What changes is what a mid-quarter number means: it's a coaching update, not a grade. See Ruling 3 above.

5

Brian's 2→10: the 2 stays — and after a real recovery, we're allowed to ask for an updated score.

Ratified 3/3

The survey truthfully measured that moment; the recovery is a different, better fact. And here's the part the math already handles (Cameron's point): if a recovered customer submits a new, higher score, it immediately counterbalances the old detractor in the same formula — no deletion needed. The score is self-repairing when recovery is real.

The reality check from the audit: of all 166 detractors in the past 12 months, not one has ever submitted a second score. Not because recovery never happens — callbacks happen ad hoc through claims today — but because asking for a GatherUp re-score doesn't appear to be part of anyone's play. We've asked customers to update public reviews before; whether anyone has ever asked for a GatherUp update we can't say for certain — but if we have, nobody's ever done it. Either way, the missing move is the same: after a genuine recovery, invite the update.

The one guardrail: the invitation comes after a real, logged recovery — never instead of one. Asking an un-recovered detractor to reconsider their score is the score-begging Reichheld watched ruin the metric elsewhere, and it stays banned.

Against

Gerald: "counting a recovered detractor punishes managers for doing exactly what you want." — It would, if the save were invisible. It isn't: the save shows up in recovery rate, and in the score itself whenever the recovered customer takes the re-score invitation. Integrity and incentive, both kept.

What we're deliberately NOT doing

No Earned Growth Rate on branch scorecards. EGR is a company-level accounting metric (revenue retention + referral-sourced growth). Its spirit is already tracked — 40% of our business is repeat/referral — and it may earn a place in company-level reporting, but it is not a quarterly branch instrument.

No standing "detractor court," and no adjudicator. Tags are logged by whoever makes the callback, in the normal claims/CX flow. A disputed tag is a low-stakes disagreement precisely because tags never change the score — it changes where a fix gets routed, so the RM settles it in the normal course and everyone moves on. The moment a tag dispute feels high-stakes, that's the tell someone is trying to make tags change what counts — see item 2's guardrail.
