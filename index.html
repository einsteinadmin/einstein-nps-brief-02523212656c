The Brittany Question

One customer scored us a 5 and texted "Stop." The Roundtable spent a morning debating whether to delete her. This is the whole argument in one place — both sides steelmanned, Reichheld fact-checked, a full year of our own data, and a proposed ruling.

1 · The case

What actually happened

Brittany A., Fort Worth, August 26. Her complete GatherUp timeline:

  • 8/24 — customer added after her move
  • 8/25 — feedback request sent by SMS, delivered. No response.
  • 8/26 — 1st reminder sent — one day later. Delivered.
  • 8/26 — she opens the survey from the reminder, selects 5 of 10, texts back "Stop," and unsubscribes from SMS.

Two true things at once: she deliberately chose a middling-negative score (an opt-out looks like texting STOP without ever opening the survey — she opened it and picked 5), and our next-day reminder cadence annoyed her into it. Her score is real. So is the feedback about our follow-up.

A 5 is a detractor, it lands in a small branch's quarterly number, and Fort Worth is already the tightest NPS story in the company. Hence the debate.

2 · The sides

What y'all are saying — steelmanned

Mike + Brian (the branch view)

Managers shouldn't be dinged for what they can't control. A single detractor moves a branch's quarter hard, and when the detractor is "I'm annoyed by your texts," coaching a team on it is impossible to defend with a straight face.

Fix the system first so this can't happen (Brian) — removal only if it happens anyway. And the credibility of a crackdown depends on the scoreboard being defensible.

Brian's second question: if a 2 gets service-recovered into a 10, does the 2 stick?

Cameron + Amanda + Matisen (the metric view)

She's a detractor and the score stays. Writing "stop" in the survey means she clicked, chose a number, and said something — deliberate acts (Matisen). Even "I hate your follow-up" is real feedback (Amanda).

Most companies don't curate their surveys. Start deleting "unfair" ones and the number stops meaning anything.

Base-rate question first (Cameron): how often does this actually happen? Nobody in the thread had a number. Now we do — Section 4.

Gerald's synthesis (via Mike)

The room is answering two different questions — "is she a detractor?" (yes) and "is this branch-attributable?" (no) — so nobody's actually disagreeing. One number is doing two jobs: measuring customer loyalty for the company and evaluating manager performance at the branch. Route, don't delete: root-cause tag every detractor, keep all of them in company NPS, count only attributable ones on the branch scorecard, add recovery-rate and response-rate as manager metrics, and fix the survey instrument so opt-outs can't enter the pipeline.

3 · The citations

What Reichheld actually says — fact-checked

Gerald's post leaned on Fred Reichheld (creator of NPS, The Ultimate Question 2.0) and his 2021 follow-up. Claim by claim:

"In 2021 he published Net Promoter 3.0, essentially conceding the metric had been corrupted" Partly holds

NP 3.0 (HBR, Nov–Dec 2021) is real, and Reichheld does describe self-reported scores getting corrupted — his named mechanism is tying scores to compensation (the car-dealer "please give me a 10" problem). He introduced Earned Growth Rate as an accounting audit to pair with NPS — not a retraction of it. He remains NPS's biggest advocate. "Wrote a follow-up book to walk it back" oversells it.

"His closed-loop doctrine requires attribution — the score is a trigger to investigate, not a verdict" Holds

Accurate. The score was never supposed to be the deliverable; the root cause was. This is the strongest borrowed idea in Gerald's post, and the proposal adopts it (tags + callbacks, Section 5).

"At ~20 responses per branch per quarter, the confidence interval is ±20 — a 90 and a 72 are indistinguishable" Breaks on our data

The statistics are right for the hypothetical n he assumed. But no Einstein branch is near n=20: our floor last quarter was Tampa at 44, the average is ~120, and Dallas had 200. At our real volumes the interval is roughly ±7, not ±20. The weakened version of his point still matters (see Section 4) — the dramatized version doesn't describe us.

"Anyone using Reichheld to defend a hard 90 threshold on a branch scorecard is invoking the practice he walked back" Partly holds

Half-true about us. NPS is on the branch scorecard — every branch graded against the 90 line, every quarter, in bands. But it is evaluation, not compensation — nobody's pay moves on NPS — and Reichheld's corruption stories are comp-linked score-begging. Worth being honest though: the gaming pressure evaluation creates doesn't show up here as score-begging. It shows up as this thread — the urge to remove scores. Which is precisely the argument for a hard no-removals rule.

Finding 1 — The problem we're debating is two reviews a year

Of 166 detractors, exactly two are artifacts a removal policy would target. That's 1.2%. Everything else is a customer with a real grievance. We nearly designed an adjudication court for two reviews a year.

Finding 2 — The reminder isn't the villain. It's half our sample.

Turning off the follow-up (floated in-thread) would roughly halve response volume — which makes the small-sample volatility everyone's complaining about worse — to prevent about one Brittany per year. The reminder-sourced detractors are also the same genuine complaints as initial-touch ones, not "stop texting me" protest votes. The tune-up worth making is Amanda's: the reminder currently fires one day after the first text. Space it 2–3 days.

The actual sequence, from GatherUp's Request Setup (checked on two branches — identical config)

Touch 1: initial rating request (Email/SMS) → Touch 2: First Rating Reminder, ON, sends 1 day after the initial → Touch 3: a "Second Rating Reminder" slot exists in GatherUp (would send 10 days after the first) but is toggled OFF on every branch. There is no third ask.

⚠️ Naming trap: the thread's "2nd follow-up" IS the First Rating Reminder — the second touch. In GatherUp's own terms the "second reminder" is already off; the thing the thread means produces half our responses. The only change on the table is the 1-day → 2–3 day spacing on the reminder that stays.

The concession Mike & Brian earn

At typical branch volume, one detractor moves the quarter 1.5–2.5 points, the noise band is ±7ish, the Green band is 3 points wide, and branches live 1–3 points off the 90 line. A single review genuinely can decide a band color at the margin. That's real, and pretending otherwise loses the room. Garland is the live case: 85 on 58 responses — one point below the Red line, one or two reviews from a different color.

The concession the data refuses

Dallas is not noise. 79 on 200 responses with 14 detractors — the most responses in the company, converging on the same score. The full-year detractor count agrees (Dallas leads with 29). The sample-size argument defends Garland's quarter; it cannot defend Dallas's. The worst score in the company is also the best-measured one.

Brian's ratio question — "for every detractor, how many promoters to move the score positive? Or is it variable?"

Both. In the NPS formula a promoter and a detractor are equal and opposite: one promoter exactly cancels one detractor (each is ±1 in the same numerator), so netting positive after a detractor lands takes two promoter responses where you'd otherwise have had passives. And yes, it's variable — how much each one moves the score depends entirely on response volume: at Houston's 273 responses a detractor costs 0.7 points; at Garland's 58 it costs 3.4. Same detractor, five times the damage — which is Finding 5 in one sentence: the denominator is the defense.

Finding 5 — The volatility fix is the response rate, and it's fully in the manager's control

Houston gets 53% of customers to respond. North Austin gets 8.7%. Same survey, same company — a 6× spread. Every point of response rate grows the denominator, shrinks the noise, and cuts the self-selection bias (angry customers respond regardless; happy ones need the nudge). You don't stabilize a branch's NPS by deleting detractors — you stabilize it by tripling the sample. Houston proves it's achievable.

Two free findings from reading all 166 comments

"The new / inexperienced mover" is the single most repeated complaint motif of the year. We named the quarter Training Camp before reading any of this. Receipts for the theme.

Three customers reported being promised follow-up that never came. The claims/callback loop is quietly manufacturing detractors on its own.

5 · The ruling

What we're proposing — and the argument against each piece

1

Nothing gets deleted. Nothing gets excluded. Company AND branch numbers keep every score.

The "unfair" population is ~1% a year. A deletion or exclusion process needs an adjudicator, invites lobbying (it started within the hour — an agent argued a score away in its branch's favor), and is the exact corruption path Reichheld documented. Fairness is handled structurally (items 3–4), not case-by-case.

Against

"Managers get dinged for what they can't control." — True at the margin, ~1% of the time. The answer is the volatility fixes below, which help every quarter, not a removal court that helps twice a year and corrodes trust in the number forever.

2

Adopt Gerald's routing layer — root-cause tag every detractor (Service / Sales-Estimate / System / Unattributed).

Tags route the fix, not the score: estimate-caused detractors feed the estimate-accuracy work, system-caused ones feed whoever owns the cadence, crew-caused ones feed coaching. Silent detractors (58% give a score only) trigger a mandatory callback attempt; only a failed callback lands them in Unattributed. This is Reichheld's closed loop, done properly.

Against

"Tags will become the exclusion filter by the back door." — The guardrail is item 1: tags never change what counts. If we ever revisit that, it's a QGR-packet ruling with one adjudicator outside the branch, not a channel debate.

3

Add the two metrics that are 100% manager-controlled: detractor recovery rate + response rate.

Gerald's best idea, upgraded by the data: recovery rate pays the Iyer save that never happened, and response rate is the structural volatility fix (Finding 5). A manager who moves response rate from 9% to 30% does more for their score's stability than any deletion policy ever could — and coaching both is coaching real behavior, not a number.

Against

"More metrics, more noise." — These replace arguing about the score with acting on it. Two numbers, both movable this week, both visible in GatherUp already.

4

Take the band question to the next QGR packet: Green is 3 points wide, the noise is ±7.

The legitimate kernel of Gerald's whole post. At current volumes, band placement below 88 is tighter than the instrument honestly measures for smaller branches. Options to evaluate at packet time: rolling two-quarter NPS for branches under ~100 responses, trend arrows next to the band, or wider W/R bands. Manifest ruling, made once, not re-litigated per review.

Against

"You're moving the goalposts for weak branches." — No score changes and the 90 line stays. This is about whether the ruler's tick marks are finer than the ruler — a measurement-honesty question, the same standard we applied to damages and the Games basis.

5

Fix the instrument: opt-out choice in the survey, carrier STOPs never enter the pipeline, reminder spaced 2–3 days.

Brian's system-first instinct, adopted in full. An explicit "stop contacting me" option gives the annoyed a lane that isn't the 0–10 scale; SMS STOP keywords get filtered from feedback text; and the next-day reminder — the thing that actually manufactured the Brittany case — moves to a 2–3 day gap. The reminder itself stays: it's half our sample at a cost of two unsubscribes a year.

Against

"Just turn the reminder off." — That halves the sample to prevent ~one artifact a year, making the volatility problem worse. The data is unambiguous here.

6

Brian's 2→10: the 2 stays. The save gets paid in recovery rate.

The survey truthfully measured that moment; the recovery is a different, better fact — and it becomes the headline of the new recovery-rate metric. Rewriting scores post-recovery builds a revision-hounding loop (GatherUp ships a "request rating revision" button that would get worn out in a month), and it's the score-begging behavior Reichheld watched ruin the metric elsewhere.

Against

Gerald: "counting a recovered detractor punishes managers for doing exactly what you want." — It would, if recovery went unmeasured. It doesn't anymore: the same act that used to be invisible becomes the metric they're praised on. Integrity and incentive, both kept.

What we're deliberately NOT doing

No Earned Growth Rate on branch scorecards. EGR is a company-level accounting metric (revenue retention + referral-sourced growth). Its spirit is already tracked — 40% of our business is repeat/referral — and it may earn a place in company-level reporting, but it is not a quarterly branch instrument.

No standing "detractor court." Tags are logged by the callback owner in the normal claims/CX flow. If a genuine dispute arises, it goes to one adjudicator outside the branch — and the score still doesn't change.
