How we handle NPS

Four rules, and the words to use when a score goes sideways. This replaces every hallway version of "does that one count?"

The short version

Every score counts. Nothing gets deleted.

We looked at a full year — 166 detractors. We found exactly two that were arguably unfair. Two, out of a year.

So we're not building a process to remove scores. The moment we start deciding which customers count, the number stops meaning anything, and every hard conversation turns into a negotiation about whether the score was fair instead of what we're going to do about it. Leadership debated this seriously and landed unanimously: nothing gets deleted, nothing gets excluded, and both the company and branch numbers keep every score.

What we are doing is fixing the two things that actually make your score feel unfair: how it gets talked about mid-quarter, and how few people answer the survey.

The four rules

1. Every score counts — company and branch.

No deletions, no exclusions, no exceptions. If a customer had a bad experience with any part of working with us — the move, the estimate, the follow-up texts, the damage claim — that's real feedback about our system, even when it isn't about your crew.

2. Mid-quarter numbers are coaching. The grade happens once, at the end.

Your NPS is only graded at the end of the quarter. Anything you hear before that is a status update so you have time to do something about it — not a verdict, and not a judgment on your branch.

If a regional manager walks in four weeks into a quarter and your score is low because of one review, that conversation should sound like "here's where you sit and here's how to move it," never "you're underperforming." If it ever sounds like the second one, push back — that's the rule, not a favor.

3. You're measured on how fast you respond — not on whether the customer cheers up.

Some upset customers will stay upset no matter what we spend, and that was never fair to hang on you. What is entirely in your control is how quickly you get back to someone who gave us a low score. That's the number that follows you.

Respond fast, log it, and the metric takes care of itself.

4. After a real fix, you can ask for an updated score.

If you actually resolved it — the claim settled, the redo happened, the refund landed — you're allowed to invite that customer to update their score. A new higher score counterbalances the old one in the same math, so a genuine save repairs the number on its own.

The hard line: you invite the re-score after a real fix, never instead of one. Asking someone to change their score without solving their problem is the thing that ruins this metric at other companies. Don't do it.

The real lever

Here's what that gap actually costs you. When only a handful of customers respond, one bad score moves your whole number and you spend the quarter at the mercy of who happened to answer. When a lot of customers respond, one detractor barely registers and your score starts reflecting your actual service.

Chasing responses from happy customers is the single most effective thing you can do to protect your NPS. It is more powerful than any policy change we could have made, and it's fully in your hands.

In practice

When a detractor lands

  1. Respond fast. Speed is the part you're measured on. Get to them before the day's out.
  2. Call, don't text. Most detractors leave a score and no comment — you won't know what happened until you ask.
  3. Go into problem-solving mode. The Fort Worth case that started this whole debate was a customer who told us in writing she'd have been fine if someone had called and worked it out. Nobody called.
  4. Log what you did so the save is visible. An unlogged recovery looks identical to no recovery.
  5. If you genuinely fixed it, invite the updated score. See rule 4.

Say this

"Here's where your NPS sits today, here's your response rate, and here's what we can do about both before this gets stamped at the end of the quarter."
"That score counts, and it's also not the whole story about your branch. Let's go fix the thing behind it."
"Your best move this month is getting more happy customers to answer the survey."

Not this

"You're underperforming." — Not mid-quarter. That's a grade, and grades happen once.
"Let's see if we can get that one thrown out." — There's no process for it, on purpose.
"Can you go change your score?" — Not without a real fix first. Ever.
